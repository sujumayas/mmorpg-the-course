# GD101 · Analysis essay brief

**Weight:** 20% of the course grade, marked out of 100.
**Work mode:** individual.
**Length:** 1,800 to 2,200 words, excluding tables, figures, the play log and the source list.
**Due:** end of week 15.

## The task

Choose one published game and explain, with the course's models and your own play evidence, how one of its rules produces one of the experiences players report. Then propose one change and predict what it would do.

The essay is a design analysis, not a review. "Is it good?" is not a question you need to answer. "Why does the last third of a game feel decided, and which rule does that?" is the kind of question you do need to answer.

### Choosing a game

- Any published board, card or digital game you can play at least twice during the term. The course library lends board and card games. Free digital games are fine.
- Not your own paper prototype, and not a game used as a main worked example in a lecture (tic-tac-toe, Monopoly, the Diablo pitch, Skirmish, Market Stall). You may compare with them.
- Name the exact version, edition or patch era you played. Rules change between editions and patches, and your analysis is about the one you played.

### What the essay must contain

1. **A question and a one-sentence answer** in the first paragraph. For example, "Why do experienced players stop taking the risky route after their first few games? Because the route's reward is fixed while its failure cost grows with the run length."
2. **Formal elements** (weeks 2 and 3). A compact description of players, objectives, procedures, rules, resources, conflict, boundaries and outcome. A table is fine.
3. **An MDA chain** (weeks 4 and 5). One aesthetic you observed or that players report, traced down to a dynamic in "who does what, when" form and to the mechanics that cause it.
4. **At least one quantitative model applied to the game** (weeks 3 to 14). Pick the one that fits your question: the lives table `W(n, L)`, the feedback-loop gap, the flow ratio, dice against deck spread, the luck dial, a payoff table, the telegraph budget, a Jaccard genre comparison or a pillar-mechanic map. State every assumption and every number you estimated.
5. **Genre position** (week 13). Use your [lab 13](../labs/lab13.md) grid, which must include your essay game as one of its five published games. Say what the game's nearest neighbour is and which genre expectation it keeps or breaks.
6. **Evidence from play.** Your own play log (appendix) from at least two sessions, with at least one session where you watched someone else play without explaining (week 7). Cite log lines in the text.
7. **A proposed change.** One rule change, the dynamic you predict, the aesthetic it protects and the one it costs. Check it for new dominant options (week 11).
8. **Sources.** Every fact about the game's rules, numbers or development cites a source with version or era. Distinguish what the game shows (a tooltip, a rulebook value) from what it computes, and mark your own measurements as yours.

## Rubric for the final essay (80 points)

| Criterion | Excellent | Good | Adequate | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Question and argument** (10) | 9-10: A specific design question answered in the first paragraph; every section serves the answer | 7-8: Clear question; one section drifts | 5-6: Question broad ("what makes it fun?") or answered only at the end | 0-4: No question; a description or review |
| **Formal elements and MDA chain** (15) | 14-15: Elements accurate and compact; the chain links a stated aesthetic to a dynamic in "who, what, when" form and to named mechanics | 11-13: Chain complete; one layer vague | 8-10: Layers confused in places (a dynamic called a mechanic, "balanced" called an aesthetic) | 0-7: No chain, or layers mixed throughout |
| **Quantitative model** (20) | 18-20: The model fits the question; inputs are measured or justified estimates; working shown; the essay says where the model breaks for this game | 14-17: Correct and relevant; assumptions partly stated | 10-13: Model applied with errors, or not connected to the question | 0-9: No model, or numbers copied from a lecture without adaptation |
| **Evidence from play** (15) | 14-15: Two or more sessions logged; one silent observation of another player; log lines cited to support claims; counts, not percentages | 11-13: Evidence present; one claim unsupported | 8-10: Play log thin or used only as illustration | 0-7: No play log, or claims rest on memory and reviews |
| **Design judgment** (10) | 9-10: One change with a predicted dynamic, the aesthetic protected and the one cost; checked for a new dominant option | 7-8: Change and prediction clear; cost or dominance check missing | 5-6: Change stated with a vague prediction | 0-4: No change, or several changes at once |
| **Sources and accuracy** (5) | 5: Every game fact cited with version or era; shown and computed values distinguished | 4: One uncited fact | 3: Several uncited facts or eras missing | 0-2: Facts wrong or unsourced |
| **Writing** (5) | 5: Plain, numbers first, within the word range, tables used where they help | 4: Readable, some padding | 3: Hard to follow in places | 0-2: Outside the word range by more than 10%, or hard to read |

Total with milestones: 5 (M1) + 15 (M2) + 80 (final) = 100.

## Rules

- **Your own words.** Paraphrase sources and cite them. Quote at most one short phrase from any one source.
- **No invented data.** Every number in the play log must come from a session that happened. Estimates are fine if you label them as estimates.
- **AI and writing tools** follow the program's current policy. Whatever tools you use, the play evidence and the calculations must be yours, and you must be able to explain any line of the essay in a short follow-up conversation if the tutor asks.
