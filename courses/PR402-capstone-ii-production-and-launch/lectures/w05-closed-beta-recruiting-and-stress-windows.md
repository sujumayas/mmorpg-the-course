## 1. What closed beta has to show

A closed beta is a test with invited players, on production servers, with the game close to its launch form. Your closed beta has three questions to answer by the end of the week:

1. **Does the server hold the evening peak?** Week 3 forecast about 56 concurrent players on a normal launch evening. Your bots found the knee at 80 players per zone server, and the normal-evening fleet has two. Bots follow scripts. Real players bunch up at the quest giver, spam the trade channel and all log in at 19:00. You want to see 56 or more real players on at once, at least once.
2. **Do the systems hold together over days?** Characters persist across sessions, the economy runs for a week, guilds form. Alpha lasted two hours. This lasts seven days.
3. **Which of your guesses were wrong?** The reach estimates from week 4, session length, where new players get stuck. Week 6 turns the answers into a balance and telemetry review.

The first question drives how many people you invite and when you ask them to play. That is the worked example.

## 2. The tester funnel

Not everyone you invite plays. Between the invite and the stress window, testers drop out at each step:

- **Invited.** A key sent, or access granted through a store's playtest system.
- **Started.** Installed and played at least one session. Many invited people never do.
- **Active.** Played more than once during the week.
- **In the window.** Online during a specific scheduled hour.

Each step keeps a share of the one before. Multiply the shares and you have the share of invites who will be in any one window. Work backwards from the number you need.

You will not know your shares until you run the beta, so start with conservative guesses and measure them from the first day. The rates in the example below are invented teaching values. Replace them with yours from the first 24 hours of the beta.

Where do testers come from? Your sign-up list from week 3, other students, local game communities, and friends of friends. Friends and classmates are useful for load but poor for feedback. They are kind, and they already know the game. Aim for at least half your testers to be strangers to the team.

## 3. Consent, data and conduct

Closed beta testers are people you are collecting data about, not load generators. Before any invite goes out:

- **Consent notice.** A short page, shown before first play, stating what telemetry you collect, why, how long you keep it and whom to contact. Follow your university's research ethics guidance if you plan to publish anything from the data. [MMO308 week 5](../../MMO308-live-operations-analytics-and-experimentation/lectures/w05-ab-testing-design.md) covers the principles.
- **Collect the minimum.** Player IDs generated by your game, not names or emails, in telemetry. The invite list with contact details stays in one place with restricted access and is deleted after the course.
- **Rules of conduct and a way to report.** A code of conduct linked from the launcher and a report command in game. [MMO305 week 12](../../MMO305-social-systems-and-online-behaviour/lectures/w12-community-management.md) covers why this matters in social systems.
- **Wipe notice.** Tell testers in writing whether characters survive to launch. Most capstone teams wipe. Say so before anyone levels.

## 4. Worked example: 150 testers and a server that never saw more than 12

### The hook

Diablo IV ran three public tests before its June 2023 launch, each over a single weekend. An early access beta for players who had pre-ordered ran from 17 to 19 March 2023. An open beta for everyone followed from 24 to 26 March. A third weekend, from 12 to 14 May, was called the Server Slam, and its stated focus was server stability.

Blizzard could have opened a beta for a month and let players drift in. It chose short, fixed weekends, and for the stability test it put the name of the goal in the title. A weekend concentrates players in time, and concurrency is what breaks servers. You have the same tool, at capstone scale. You cannot make more people want to play your game this week. You can decide when they play.

### The model

```
active testers   A = invites × p_start
window CCU       = A × p_window
invites needed   = ceil( ceil(target / p_window) / p_start )

without windows:
average CCU      = A × sessions × hours / 168
peak CCU         ≈ average CCU × peak ratio

exposure         E = A × sessions × hours          (player-hours in the week)
clean beta       rate < 3 / E                      (rule of three, 95%)
```

- `p_start` is the share of invited testers who play at least once.
- `p_window` is the share of active testers who join one scheduled window.
- `target` is the concurrency you need to see, here the week 3 evening peak.
- `sessions` and `hours` are sessions per tester per week and hours per session.
- 168 is the hours in a week.

The last two lines come from [MMO407 week 3](../../MMO407-launch-scaling-and-post-mortems/lectures/w03-beta-strategies.md) and are not re-derived here.

### Numbers you can check

Emberfall wants to see 60 real players online together, a little above its evening peak forecast of 56. The team assumes `p_start = 0.45` and `p_window = 0.40`, and testers who play three sessions of 1.5 hours each in the week.

| Step | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Active testers needed | ceil(60 / 0.40) | 150 |
| Invites needed | ceil(150 / 0.45) | 334 |
| Sessions in the week | 150 × 3 | 450 |

The 450 sessions are the number week 4 used for triage. The two weeks share one plan.

**Without windows.** If the same 150 testers play whenever they like, the week holds `150 × 3 × 1.5 = 675` player-hours spread over 168 hours. Average concurrency is `675 / 168 = 4.0` players, and even with a peak ratio of 3 the busiest evening sees about 12. The server never meets more than a fifth of its launch-evening load. Every performance problem between 12 and 56 players stays hidden until launch.

**With windows.** The team schedules two 90-minute stress windows, Tuesday and Thursday at 19:00, announced in the invite and reminded by message the day before. With `p_window = 0.4`, each window expects 60 players. If Tuesday's window reaches only 35, the team knows by Wednesday morning and can send a second invite batch before Thursday.

How much does the funnel matter? The invites needed for the same 150 active testers:

| `p_start` | Invites |
|---|---|
| 0.30 | 500 |
| 0.45 | 334 |
| 0.60 | 250 |

A team that assumes 0.6 and gets 0.3 has half the testers it planned for, and a stress window of 30. That is why invites go out in batches, as in the next section.

**What a clean week proves.** The week's exposure is `E = 675` player-hours. If a bug never appears, the rule of three says only that its rate is below `3 / 675`, about once per 225 player-hours. Week 3 forecast launch week at `300 × 1.5 × 7 = 3,150` player-hours. That is 4.7 times the beta. A bug the beta never showed could still fire up to `3 × 3,150 / 675 = 14` times in launch week. Closed beta finds the common problems and the concurrency problems. It does not certify the rare ones. The open beta in week 8 adds exposure, and your launch plan must still assume some bugs will first appear at launch.

This program reproduces the numbers:

```python
import math

target = 60            # humans online at once in a stress window (week 3 evening peak was 56)
p_start = 0.45         # invited testers who install and play at least one session
p_window = 0.40        # active testers who join a given scheduled window
sessions, hours = 3, 1.5

active = math.ceil(target / p_window)
invites = math.ceil(active / p_start)
print(f"active testers needed {active}, invites needed {invites}")

spread_avg = active * sessions * hours / 168
print(f"no windows: average CCU {spread_avg:.1f}, evening peak about {spread_avg * 3:.0f}")

exposure = active * sessions * hours
launch = 300 * 1.5 * 7
print(f"beta week player-hours {exposure:.0f}; clean beta bound: rate < 1 per {exposure / 3:.0f} h")
print(f"launch week player-hours {launch:.0f}; unseen-bug firings allowed at launch up to {3 * launch / exposure:.1f}")
for p in (0.30, 0.45, 0.60):
    print(f"p_start {p:.2f}: invites {math.ceil(active / p)}")
```

It prints:

```
active testers needed 150, invites needed 334
no windows: average CCU 4.0, evening peak about 12
beta week player-hours 675; clean beta bound: rate < 1 per 225 h
launch week player-hours 3150; unseen-bug firings allowed at launch up to 14.0
p_start 0.30: invites 500
p_start 0.45: invites 334
p_start 0.60: invites 250
```

### Cross-game recognition

- **Diablo IV, 2023.** Three weekends, one of them named for server stability. The windows in this guide are the same idea at a smaller scale: fix the time, fill the servers, watch what breaks.
- **Steam Playtest.** Valve's playtest system puts a "join the playtest" request on the store page. Developers grant access in batches from the dashboard, and Steam picks testers at random from those who requested. That is the funnel above made into a tool. You grant one batch, measure `p_start`, and grant the next with a better number. A playtest can issue up to 50,000 keys and runs as a separate app, so its reviews and playtime do not touch the main game.
- **SimCity, 2013.** [MMO407 week 3](../../MMO407-launch-scaling-and-post-mortems/lectures/w03-beta-strategies.md) tells how a beta run to test load did not predict launch load. The exposure calculation above is the reason. A beta holds a small fraction of launch play, however well it is run.

### Eyes-open judgment

Windows cost something. A tester who can only play at 22:00 misses both, and players who join a window play a crowded, stressful version of your game that is not how most sessions will feel. Keep the windows short and few, two in the week, and let testers play freely the rest of the time. Analyze window sessions separately from free sessions in week 6, or the crowding will distort your session-length and difficulty data.

Inviting more people is not free either. Every active tester adds support messages and bug reports to the ops tax. 334 invites is a number a team of seven can support for a week with a FAQ and a triage rota. 1,000 is not.

Do not replace real players with bots to reach the target. Bots test the server's capacity. The point of the window is to see what real players do that bots do not. If the window falls short, fill the gap with bots and say so in the report. Do not report the combined number as real concurrency.

## 5. Running the week

- **Batch the invites.** Send about 60% of the planned invites on day 1. Measure `p_start` after 24 hours and size the second batch to hit the target before the first window.
- **Daily triage.** Fifteen minutes each morning with the week 4 model. Update reach estimates with the telemetry events you added. Gate bugs found in beta get fixed and deployed through staging the same day if possible.
- **One feedback channel.** A single form or forum channel, linked in game. Scattered feedback in private messages is lost feedback. [MMO308 week 10](../../MMO308-live-operations-analytics-and-experimentation/lectures/w10-player-feedback-loops-forums-surveys-polls.md) covers survey design.
- **Watch the windows live.** During each window, one person watches server dashboards (tick time, memory, queue length) and one watches chat. Record the peak CCU and any tick-time spike with its time.
- **Keep the ops log.** Every hour spent on beta support, deploys and fixes is tagged `ops` (lab 01). This week's measured tax is the most important number for replanning weeks 6 and 7.

## Common failure modes

- **Open-ended beta, no windows.** The server never sees more than a dozen players, and the team calls it "stable".
- **Inviting only friends.** Load arrives, feedback does not. Friends are polite and already know the controls.
- **One big invite batch.** A wrong funnel guess cannot be corrected before the windows. Send in batches.
- **No consent notice.** Telemetry collected without telling testers is a problem for the course and for the university. It is also avoidable in ten minutes.
- **Counting bots as testers.** The report says 60 concurrent, and 40 were scripts. Report the two numbers apart.
- **Reading a clean week as proof.** 675 player-hours cannot show a rare bug is absent. Say what the bound is.
- **Nobody watching the window.** The tick spike at 19:40 is in the logs, but nobody looked at the dashboard and nobody knows what players were doing at the time.
