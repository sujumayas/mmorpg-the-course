## 1. A falling-block game and a fight about method

In 1997 Janet Murray published *Hamlet on the Holodeck*, one of the first serious books about stories in digital media. In a short passage she read Tetris as a story about modern life. The falling blocks, she argued, act out the experience of busy Americans in the 1990s. Tasks keep arriving, you fit them into an overcrowded schedule, you clear space, and more arrive. She called the game a "perfect enactment" of that life.

Four years later, in the first issue of the journal *Game Studies*, Markku Eskelinen used that passage as his prime example of what was going wrong. His point was simple. People outside universities have no trouble telling a game from a story. If someone throws you a ball, you catch it; you do not wait for it to start narrating. Yet academics, he argued, kept treating games as a kind of literature or film because those were the tools they already had. Murray's reading of Tetris told you about Murray's reading habits, not about Tetris. Tetris has no characters. Its events change the state of play (a line clears, the stack grows) without telling anyone anything.

You can feel both sides. Murray is right that people find meaning in abstract games. Eskelinen is right that "the blocks mean my to-do list" ignores almost everything that makes Tetris Tetris, such as the rotation rules, the speed curve, the fact that you lose when the stack reaches the top. A reading that would work equally well for a painting of falling blocks has skipped the game.

That argument, repeated and sharpened over about five years, is known as the **ludology vs narratology debate**. It is the founding quarrel of game studies as an academic field, and it left behind a method you will use in every close reading you write.

## 2. The two positions

### Narratology

**Narratology** is the study of how stories work: events, characters, the order and speed of telling, who tells. It began in literary theory. Scholars who came to games from literature, film and theatre naturally asked narratological questions. Who is the protagonist? What is the plot? How does the game handle point of view? Murray's book is the best-known example. Its hope was that computers would become a new medium for storytelling, in the way the novel and cinema were.

Nobody in the debate called themselves a "narratologist" as a banner. The label was mostly applied by the other side to anyone who treated games primarily as stories.

### Ludology

**Ludology** (from Latin *ludus*, game) is the study of games as games: rules, goals, player actions, win and loss states. Gonzalo Frasca proposed the term in a 1999 essay, "Ludology Meets Narratology," first published in Finnish. He wanted a discipline that studies game and play activities on their own terms, independent of the medium, the way narratology studies stories independent of whether they are told in a novel or a film.

The ludologists (Frasca, Eskelinen, Juul and Espen Aarseth, whose 1997 book *Cybertext* supplied much of the vocabulary) made three connected claims:

1. **Games are not a kind of story.** They can contain stories, but their defining features are rules and player action.
2. **Stories do not translate into games the way they translate between other media.** Juul's 2001 essay makes this the central argument. A story can move from novel to film and remain recognizably the same story. A game cannot be retold as a novel and remain a game, because the novel drops the thing that made it a game, which is the rules you act within.
3. **Using literary tools on games risks "colonising" them**, Eskelinen's word. You end up studying the cutscenes and the box text and missing the play.

### What the debate actually was

By 2003 Frasca was arguing, in a paper with the title "Ludologists love stories, too: notes from a debate that never took place," that the fight had been exaggerated. Few ludologists said stories did not matter. Few narrative-minded scholars said rules did not matter. Much of the heat came from each side attacking a weaker version of the other.

Two syntheses came out of this period and are what you should carry forward.

**Jenkins's narrative architecture (2004).** Henry Jenkins argued that game designers are less like storytellers and more like theme-park designers or architects. They build spaces that can do narrative work in four ways. A space can **evoke** stories you already know (a pirate cove evokes every pirate film). It can be a stage where story events are **enacted** by the player. It can **embed** story information in its objects and layout for the player to piece together (the corpse, the diary, the barricaded door). And it can supply resources for **emergent** stories that players create through play and retell afterward.

**Juul's half-real (2005).** In *Half-Real*, Jesper Juul describes video games as two things at once. The rules are real: you really do lose if the stack reaches the top, and you really did win the match. The world is fictional: there is no actual dragon. The player moves between both layers constantly, and the interesting questions are about how the two layers relate.

That second idea is the one this lecture turns into a tool. If a game has a rules layer and a fiction layer, you can read each layer separately and then ask whether they say the same thing.

## 3. Worked example: BioShock's Little Sisters and the price of mercy

### The hook

BioShock (2K Boston and 2K Australia, 2007) is set in Rapture, an underwater city founded on the principle that each person should serve their own interest. You gain powers by spending ADAM, a resource carried by Little Sisters, young girls who have been altered to gather it. When you reach one, the game gives you a choice. **Harvest** her and you get 160 ADAM; the fiction frames this as killing a child. **Rescue** her and you get 80 ADAM; she is cured and freed.

So the rules pay you twice as much to do what the story tells you is monstrous. That looks like a clear case of the game's two layers pulling in opposite directions. In October 2007 the designer Clint Hocking published a blog critique of BioShock that named exactly this kind of split **ludonarrative dissonance**: a conflict between what a game is about as a game and what it is about as a story. The term stuck and is now used in reviews everywhere.

Before you repeat the phrase, check the numbers. Does BioShock really pay you double to harvest?

### The model

Split any meaningful choice in a game into two layers and record each separately. This is the **rules-fiction ledger**.

```
Rules layer:   payoff(option), counted in the game's own resource
Fiction layer: framing(option) = +1 (presented as right), -1 (presented as wrong), 0 (neutral)

Incentive ratio  I = total payoff(option A) / total payoff(option B)
```

- **Option A** is the option the fiction frames as wrong. **Option B** is the one it frames as right.
- **Total payoff** is counted over the span the player actually decides over. For a choice repeated through the whole game, that span is the whole game, not one encounter.
- `I > 1` means the rules pay more for the act the fiction condemns. `I = 1` means the rules are indifferent. `I < 1` means the rules pay more for the act the fiction praises.

The ledger then gives three readings:

| Rules say | Fiction says | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong act pays clearly less (`I` well below 1) | Wrong act is wrong | **Consonant**: both layers push the same way |
| Wrong act pays clearly more (`I` well above 1) | Wrong act is wrong | **Tense**: the layers disagree. If the designers intend it, it is a moral test. If not, it is dissonance |
| `I` close to 1 | Wrong act is wrong | **Neutralized**: the rules have taken the cost out of the choice, so only the fiction decides |

Notice that the model does not tell you which reading is good. It tells you which one you are looking at. The critical judgment comes after.

### Numbers you can check

BioShock has 21 Little Sisters you can harvest or rescue. The per-encounter rules are the ones in the hook: 160 ADAM for a harvest, 80 for a rescue. There is one more rule, and it changes everything. **After every three rescues, the scientist who cures the girls leaves you a gift containing 200 ADAM**, plus tonics (passive upgrades) and a plasmid (an active power) you cannot get any other way.

Per encounter, the incentive ratio is

```
I_encounter = 160 / 80 = 2.0
```

But the player decides 21 times, so count the whole game. Let `h` be the number you harvest and `r = 21 − h` the number you rescue. Gifts come in whole numbers, one per three rescues:

```
ADAM(h) = 160h + 80r + 200 × floor(r / 3)
```

Check the two pure strategies by hand:

- Harvest all 21: `160 × 21 = 3,360` ADAM.
- Rescue all 21: `80 × 21 + 200 × 7 = 1,680 + 1,400 = 3,080` ADAM.

```
I_game = 3,360 / 3,080 ≈ 1.09
```

The gap is 280 ADAM, about 8% of the harvester's total, and the rescuer gets unique items the harvester never sees. A rescue is really worth `80 + 200/3 ≈ 147` ADAM once you spread the gift across the three rescues that earn it. The per-encounter view says harvesting pays 100% more. The whole-game view says it pays about 9% more in ADAM and less in items.

Mixed strategies behave oddly because of the floor. The table assumes you find and open every gift you earn, which the wiki's totals also assume:

| Harvested (h) | Rescued (r) | Gifts | Total ADAM | vs rescue-all |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 21 | 7 | 3,080 | 0 |
| 1 | 20 | 6 | 2,960 | −120 |
| 2 | 19 | 6 | 3,040 | −40 |
| 3 | 18 | 6 | 3,120 | +40 |
| 6 | 15 | 5 | 3,160 | +80 |
| 12 | 9 | 3 | 3,240 | +160 |
| 18 | 3 | 1 | 3,320 | +240 |
| 21 | 0 | 0 | 3,360 | +280 |

Look at row 2. A player who rescues every girl except one ends up with **less** ADAM than one who rescues all 21, because that single harvest breaks a set of three and costs a 200-ADAM gift. When harvests come in threes, each block of three adds only 40 ADAM (480 from harvesting against 440 from rescuing three and collecting the gift).

### A visual you can move

Open the interactive. The left panel shows one encounter, a bar of 160 next to a bar of 80. That is the view the player gets in the moment, and it screams "twice as much." Without the gifts, BioShock's choice is strongly tense. With them, the choice is close to neutralized.

### Cross-game recognition

The ledger is one idea you can carry to any game with a choice the fiction cares about. Three rewrites follow.

**BioShock 2 (2K Marin, 2010).** The sequel keeps the choice but reworks the reward, adding an adopt-and-gather routine and gifts every four rescues. The BioShock Wiki's totals are 3,330 ADAM for a full harvester and 3,240 for a full rescuer who completes every gathering. That gives `I_game = 3,330 / 3,240 ≈ 1.03`. The designers pushed the rules even closer to indifference. By the sequel, the harvest choice is almost entirely a fiction choice.

**Papers, Please (Lucas Pope, 2013).** You are a border inspector. Week 12 built the full money model for this game. Here you need only its core. The rules pay you 5 credits for each entrant you process correctly by the day's regulations. If you let someone through who lacks a document, you get a citation. You lose that entrant's 5 credits, and from the third citation of a day onward you pay a penalty as well. At the end of each day you pay rent and choose whether to buy food and heat for your family. The fiction regularly presents entrants whose papers are wrong but whose case is sympathetic. So option A (follow the rules and turn them away) is framed as cold, and option B (show mercy) is framed as kind. On the ledger, B pays 0 or less and A pays 5, so the kind act always costs you. That is tense on purpose. The game wants mercy to cost your family something, and the tension is the whole design. Same ledger as BioShock, opposite intent.

**Undertale (Toby Fox, 2015).** Killing a monster gives you EXP, and enough EXP raises your LV, which raises your HP and attack. That is the standard role-playing contract, in which violence pays. Late in the game, a character tells you what the acronyms stand for: EXP is "execution points" and LV is "Level of Violence." Here the rules pay for the act the fiction condemns, as in BioShock, but the fiction then turns and names the payment itself as the problem. The ledger reads tense, and the game makes the tension its subject. You cannot call it dissonance, because the game is fully aware of it.

**Tetris, read with the ledger.** Run Murray's reading through the model and it fails at the first step. There is no option the fiction frames as right or wrong, because Tetris has almost no fiction. Every "meaning" in Murray's reading is supplied by the critic, not by either layer of the game. That is Eskelinen's objection stated as a test rather than an insult.

### Eyes-open design judgment

The ledger shows that "the rules reward the bad act" is only the start of a reading. Four judgments follow.

- **Per-encounter and whole-game incentives are different claims.** A player facing one Little Sister, with no knowledge of the gifts, experiences a 2:1 temptation. A player on a second playthrough knows it is close to even. Hocking's essay describes exactly the first experience: he assumed the girls could not be saved, and harvesting felt mechanically correct. Both readings are true of different players. Say which player you mean.
- **Neutralizing a choice is a design decision with a cost.** BioShock's designers could have kept the 2:1 ratio and made the choice a real sacrifice, as Papers, Please does. Instead they balanced it so neither path is punished, which protects players who choose mercy from a weaker character. The cost is that the "moral choice" is no longer a test of anything the rules can measure. It becomes a choice of which ending and which cutscenes you see.
- **Tension is not a flaw by itself.** Papers, Please and Undertale both have rules that pay for the act the fiction dislikes. Neither is dissonant in Hocking's sense, because the tension is the thing the game is about. Dissonance is tension the work does not seem to know it has.
- **Hocking's actual target was bigger than the Little Sisters.** Read the essay. He argues that the harvesting mechanic expresses the game's theme well. His complaint is about a second, larger contract: the story forces you to help a character called Atlas, and you have no rules-level way to refuse, which contradicts the self-interest the rules taught you. The ledger for that contract has only one column of options, which is its own kind of problem. A critic who cites "ludonarrative dissonance" and points only at the Little Sisters has not read the source.

### Check the model

1. A game offers a choice 12 times. Stealing pays 50 gold; paying pays 20 gold, plus a 90-gold reward after every four honest choices. Compute `I_encounter` and `I_game` for pure strategies, and classify the choice.
2. In BioShock, a player harvests exactly 4 Little Sisters. Compute their total ADAM and compare it with rescue-all.
3. Suppose a patch removed BioShock's gifts but raised the rescue payoff to 140. What are `I_encounter` and `I_game` now? What changes for the player facing one Little Sister?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. `I_encounter = 50 / 20 = 2.5`. Steal all: `12 × 50 = 600`. Pay all: `12 × 20 + 3 × 90 = 240 + 270 = 510`. `I_game = 600 / 510 ≈ 1.18`. Tense per encounter, milder over the game. Whether the designers meant that depends on whether players know about the reward.
2. `h = 4`, `r = 17`, gifts `= floor(17/3) = 5`. ADAM `= 640 + 1,360 + 1,000 = 3,000`. That is 80 less than rescue-all.
3. `I_encounter = 160 / 140 ≈ 1.14`. `I_game = 3,360 / 2,940 ≈ 1.14`. The two views now agree. The player facing one girl sees the true, small price of mercy at the moment of choice, and no longer has to trust an unseen reward.

## 4. Beyond the ledger: what else each side taught us

The ledger is a ludological tool with a narrative question inside it. The debate left more than that.

### From the ludologists: the simulation is an argument

Ian Bogost's *Persuasive Games* (2007), which you read for week 12, extends ludology into a theory of meaning. He calls it **procedural rhetoric**: games make arguments through the processes they run, not only through words and pictures. A game where farming always fails unless you borrow money makes an argument about debt, whether or not any character mentions it. You met a version of this in GS101 week 1 with The Landlord's Game, a board game built in the early twentieth century to argue against land monopoly. Procedural rhetoric gives the ledger's rules column its full weight. The rules are not neutral machinery under the story. They are a second text that can agree with the first, contradict it, or say something the first never mentions.

### From the narrative side: space tells stories too

Jenkins's embedded narrative explains a large part of how games from the 2000s onward tell stories without cutscenes. The skeleton in the bathtub with a note beside it is a story the player assembles by walking through a room. Designers call this **environmental storytelling**. It lives in the fiction layer, but it is delivered through a rules-layer activity (exploration), so it sits exactly where the two traditions meet.

Emergent narrative matters most for the MMOs this program specializes in. The stories players tell about a guild's first raid kill, a betrayal or a market crash were not written by anyone. They come out of the rules meeting other people. A pure narratologist has no author to study there, and a pure ludologist has no text. You need both.

### A sixth step for your close readings

GS101 week 1 gave you five steps: describe, find the machine, name the experience, place it in context, make one claim. Add this between steps 3 and 4:

**Check the layers.** Pick one choice or action the fiction cares about. Fill in the ledger: what the rules pay for each option, over the span the player decides over, and how the fiction frames each. Classify it as consonant, tense or neutralized. Then ask whether the game seems to know which one it is.

A reading that makes a claim about a game's meaning without checking what the rules pay for is writing about the fiction only. It may still be interesting, but it has skipped half the object, which is what Eskelinen accused Murray of doing.

## In-class activity (25 min)

In groups of three, build a rules-fiction ledger for one choice in a game you know well. Each group picks a different game, and at least one group should pick a multiplayer or MMO game.

Materials: the ledger template on the board (columns: option, rules payoff per encounter, rules payoff over the relevant span, fiction framing, source of each number), and laptops for looking up numbers on a wiki.

1. (5 min) Choose a choice the fiction takes a position on: stealing, sparing an enemy, betraying an ally, killing a neutral creature, spending real money.
2. (10 min) Fill in both payoff columns with numbers. If you cannot find a number, write "unknown" and say how you would measure it in play. Record the source and game version for each number.
3. (5 min) Compute `I_encounter` and `I_game` if you have the numbers. Classify the choice.
4. (5 min) Write one sentence of judgment: is the relationship consonant, deliberately tense, dissonant or neutralized, and what evidence tells you whether the designers intended it?

Expected output: one filled ledger row per option, two ratios (or a measurement plan), a classification and a one-sentence judgment. Two groups present in the last minutes of class.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Ludologists thought stories in games don't matter."** Almost none argued that. Their claim was that stories are not what makes a game a game, and that literary tools alone miss the rules. Frasca's 2003 paper says so in its title.
- **"Ludonarrative dissonance means the gameplay and story are different."** Every game's rules and fiction differ in some way. Dissonance is a specific claim: the rules reward what the fiction condemns (or the reverse), and the work does not seem to intend it. Papers, Please and Undertale have tension without dissonance.
- **"The per-encounter reward tells you what the rules want."** Only if the player decides once. For repeated choices, count the whole span. BioShock's 2:1 becomes about 1.09:1.
- **"The debate was settled, so you can ignore it."** The debate faded, but its method did not. Every time a reviewer says a game's systems "undercut its story," they are making a ludological argument, and you should be able to check it.
