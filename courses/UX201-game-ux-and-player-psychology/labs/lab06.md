# UX201 · Lab 06: Two instruments, one audience

Assigned in week 6 ([lecture](../lectures/w06-player-motivation-models.md)). Due at the start of week 7. Pairs; each pair submits one report.

## Objective

Measure the motivations of one group of people twice, once with rating-scale items and once with forced-choice items, and compare the two pictures. You will see in your own data whether forced choice manufactures trade-offs between types, how many people have no single primary motivation, and how much the answer to "what type are our players?" depends on the instrument. Then you turn the comparison into advice for a fictional studio.

## Starter materials

**1. Item bank.** [`lab06-starter/item_bank.md`](lab06-starter/item_bank.md). Sixteen rating-scale statements (four per type, rated 1 to 5) and twelve forced-choice pairs (each of the six type pairs appears twice, so each type appears in six items). All items are written for this course. The file also gives the exact instructions to show respondents and the data-entry format.

**2. Scoring script.** [`lab06-starter/score_motivation.py`](lab06-starter/score_motivation.py), standard-library Python 3. For each respondent it prints the rating mean per type, the forced-choice percentage per type (wins ÷ 6), the sum of the forced-choice scores (always 200%), the primary motivation under Yee's rule applied to the rating means, and the top forced-choice type. For the whole group it prints the Pearson correlation between every pair of types in each format and the share of respondents with no primary. Option: `--factor` (the primary rule's factor, default 0.75).

**3. Example data.** [`lab06-starter/motivation_data.csv`](lab06-starter/motivation_data.csv) holds 12 invented respondents generated by a simulation, so you can run the script before collecting anything. Replace the rows with your own data and keep the header.

**4. Survey form.** Build the two parts as a short online form or on paper. Put the rating-scale part first, then the forced-choice part, so the forced choices don't prime the ratings. Randomise the order of the forced-choice pairs and of the two options within each pair for each respondent. Most form tools can shuffle questions. On paper, prepare two or three orderings.

**5. Consent line (put it at the top of the form).**

> This short survey (about 5 minutes) is for a university class exercise on game motivation. It is anonymous. You can skip it or stop at any time. Your answers will be reported only as group results.

## Steps

1. **Pilot** the form on one person. Time it and fix anything they misread.
2. **Collect at least 12 responses** from people who play games at least occasionally. Aim for a mix of ages if you can, and record only an age band.
3. **Enter the data** in the CSV format and run the script. Then run it again with `--factor 0.6` and `--factor 0.9`.
4. **Compare correlations.** For each type pair, put the rating-scale `r` next to the forced-choice `r`. Is the forced-choice mean near −0.33? Which pairs change sign between formats?
5. **Find the mismatches.** List respondents whose top forced-choice type differs from their top rating type, or who have no primary under the rating rule but a clear forced-choice winner. Pick the two most striking and explain each in terms of lecture section 2 (level against order, near-ties).
6. **Sample-size check.** With `n = 12`, a correlation needs to be about ±0.58 before it is unlikely to be chance at the usual 5% level. Say which of your correlations clear that bar, and what that means for any claim about your group.
7. **Advise.** A fictional studio making a co-op exploration game ran a forced-choice survey of its community and concluded that "only 10% are Socialisers, so co-op is a low priority." Using your comparison, write a one-paragraph response.

## Deliverable

A report of at most two pages plus appendices.

- Who responded (how many, how recruited, age bands) and the form order you used.
- A table of the six type pairs with the rating-scale and forced-choice correlations side by side.
- The share with no primary motivation at each factor, and how it compares with Yee's 43%.
- The two mismatched respondents, explained.
- The sample-size paragraph.
- The paragraph of advice to the studio.
- Appendices: the data file (no names) and the three script outputs.
